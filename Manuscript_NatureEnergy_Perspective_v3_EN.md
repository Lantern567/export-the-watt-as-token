## Abstract

The cleanest, cheapest power is increasingly built where almost no one can use it—remote mega-hydropower, desert wind and solar, and curtailment-throttled existing plants—whose stranded output already sums to a major economy's annual electricity use. Meanwhile, artificial intelligence has created an industrial load unusually indifferent to where it runs: as long as its output ships as tokens (information) rather than current, it can be consumed around the clock in any time zone. We argue that, for these stranded resources, the highest-value export is not the electron but the token—deploying inference compute at the resource and sending its output over fibre. The demand's global diurnal peak-to-mean ratio is only ~1.33; a longitudinally distributed portfolio, run as a "follow-the-sun" relay, lets ~90% of demand be met directly by coincident clean supply with no storage, and needs far less battery than any single site to run 24/7. A source kWh packaged as tokens carries one-to-three orders of magnitude more exportable value than as an electron; yet bottom-up life-cycle accounting shows cheap power lowers the per-token cost by only single-digit percent—the real win is monetising near-zero-value curtailed power and avoiding the vast capital of UHV export. The constraint that paces the build-out is neither energy nor water, but chips.

## Main

Around the world, the lowest-cost clean energy is being built where no one can consume it. China's Yarlung Tsangpo project will generate ~300 TWh a year in a Himalayan gorge—about three times the Three Gorges Dam—more than 2,000 km from load centres¹,². But it is only the most extreme example of a global pattern. Grand Inga on the Congo, desert wind-solar export hubs from the Pilbara to Kazakhstan to Namibia, and the Gobi renewable mega-bases each gather tens to hundreds of TWh of generation far from load; meanwhile **existing** wind and solar plants in western China, West Texas, northern Chile, South Australia and Scotland already curtail double-digit TWh each year³. The conventional frame treats all of this as a transmission problem to be solved with more lines. That frame is not wrong, but it is increasingly the **wrong default**—because the same decade that produced these resources also produced a demand that can be consumed elsewhere and shipped out as information. We therefore invert the narrative: we first ask what this demand is, then which stranded resources can serve it, in what form most profitably, and what ultimately limits its build-out.

We advance four progressive quantitative steps, each clearing one objection for the next: on the **demand** side, the AI token load is vast, global, continuous and location-insensitive (Fig. 2); on the **supply-and-matching** side, a tiered global stranded portfolio rivals that demand in magnitude (Fig. 3b), and a longitudinally distributed fleet can follow it with far less storage than any single site (Fig. 3c,d; Box 1); on the **economics** side, the token is the value-maximising export by both value density and life-cycle cost (Fig. 4); and on the **constraint** side, what paces the build-out is chips and capital, not watts (Fig. 5).

### A global, continuous, location-insensitive industrial load

Global data-centre and AI electricity use is rising steeply⁴: the International Energy Agency (IEA) projects ~1 PWh/yr of AI electricity by 2030, while longer-range extrapolations push it to several PWh and beyond (we give three explicitly illustrative trajectories reaching ~2.1, 5.3 and 21.9 PWh/yr by 2050; Fig. 2b). Its unit of account, the token, has become both enormous in aggregate and, for the first time, credibly measurable in energy intensity⁵,⁶.

But what makes this demand special is its **space–time structure**. First, it is **location-insensitive**: as long as the workload is batchable and delay-tolerant—large-scale pre-training, batch and chained inference, synthetic-data generation, agentic background tasks—it does not depend on proximity to users and can be routed to wherever surplus clean power is. Second, it is temporally **ubiquitous**: inference is consumed around the clock in every time zone. We weight six macro-regions (North America, Europe-Africa, East Asia, South Asia, South America, Oceania) by their AI-usage share²²⁻²⁵ (Fig. 2a); each region's local diurnal activity (its shape empirically fit to real hourly web-activity data²⁶, peaking ~21:00 local and troughing in the small hours), staggered by its own time zone, is summed. The resulting global demand envelope has a diurnal peak-to-mean ratio of ~**1.33**, far flatter than any single region (single-site diurnal ~1.5–2.6; Fig. 2c–e)—one region's evening peak is offset by another's late-night trough. And 1.33 is already a **conservative** figure: this diurnal shape is not assumed—it is taken from real hourly activity data and independently validated against a real LLM-inference request log²⁷ (whose diurnal P2M of 1.69 closely matches our activity kernel's 1.70; Pearson 0.87 after phase alignment; Fig. S2d); moreover we treat it as a **rigid load that must be met in real time**, whereas such workloads are in fact batchable, delay-tolerant and can actively shift to track supply—so real operation only makes the matching easier, not harder. Across multiple time granularities (Fig. 2), demand has limited diurnal swing, almost no seasonality, and only a slowly rising multi-year trend. It is precisely this "location-insensitive + temporally continuous" duality that makes the later move—stitching dispersed stranded supply together to follow it—possible.

### A stranded portfolio on the scale of AI demand, and a global fleet that follows it

How big is the opportunity? We assembled a tiered, large stranded clean-energy portfolio (Box 1; 35 sites) and screened it by what an on-site, co-located compute terminal actually needs—capacity factor, cooling climate, latency to a fibre landing point, distance to infrastructure—following the method of ref. 7. **Tier 1** is discrete giant projects (remote mega-hydro + planned desert export hubs), totalling ~1,100–2,150 TWh/yr of strandable energy; **Tier 2** is **existing** wind/solar plants whose output is already curtailed today at near-zero marginal value, adding ~60–110 TWh/yr. The total, ~**1.2–2.3 PWh/yr**, is deliberately not exaggerated: it is the **same order of magnitude** as the IEA's ~1 PWh/yr projection for 2030 AI electricity (about 1.2–2.3×; Fig. 3b), and the theoretical desert potential is several times larger again⁷. Our claim is not that a single resource overwhelms the world, but that the global stranded base and the demand it could serve are the same order of magnitude—so the decision of "where the next gigawatts of compute are built" is implicitly the decision of "which stranded watts get monetised".

And the physical network needed to connect these resources to demand **already exists**. The submarine-and-terrestrial fibre backbone already blankets the world's coasts (Fig. 3a), reducing the connection problem for a stranded site to a single terrestrial fibre spur from a landing station to the inland site—at a cost of only tens of thousands of dollars per km, in stark contrast to the millions per km of a transmission corridor⁷,²¹. Figure 3a overlays three things on one world map: resource quality, existing fibre connectivity, and our 35 specific candidate sites.

A single site sees only its own sun and wind, but the world's token demand is not hostage to any one site. This points to a solution different from "using storage to make each site individually 24/7"⁷: disperse supply along longitude so that the **portfolio**—not any single site—follows this globe-spanning load. Using real hourly capacity factors from representative sites spanning −102° to +138° longitude (ERA5 reanalysis¹⁴), we built a transparent "follow-the-sun" relay schedule. The result: for a flexibly routable, delay-tolerant load, ~**90%** of round-the-clock global demand can be met directly by **coincident** clean supply with **no storage at all**; with a firm hydro anchor in the portfolio this rises to ~**93%** (Fig. 3c), the remainder absorbed by batchable buffering in adjacent hours and only ~0.1% a true deficit. An independent linear-programming upper bound confirms the result is robust (the relay rule reaches ~**99.9%** of optimal), with no reliance on a complex optimiser.

Geographic balance is itself a cheaper design lever than storage. A PV-dominated single site has a diurnal peak-to-mean of ~2.6 with a large diurnal swing; a geographically balanced portfolio of the same sites drops to ~1.3—because one region's afternoon is another's midnight. In battery terms: even in the worst case where clean supply just equals mean demand, a single PV site still needs ~**7.8 hours** of mean-load battery to run 24/7, whereas a longitudinally dispersed portfolio drops to **<1 hour** (Fig. 3d)—what does the firming is the dispersion of supply, not battery capacity; a portfolio tilted only toward the largest resources (which cluster in the Eastern Hemisphere) smooths less, needing ~2.7 hours. A multi-week **seasonal** residual remains—a genuine limitation—carried not by batteries but by the portfolio's firm hydro members and by letting batchable utilisation flex seasonally.

Placing this matching on the timeline (Fig. 3e), one more point is essential: **the true ceiling on stranded supply is not today's named projects but the future developable clean-energy potential.** The literature gives **technical potential exceeding 100 PWh/yr each** for solar and wind, hundreds to tens of thousands of PWh/yr for all renewables, with even the cost-screened **economic potential already exceeding current and near-term demand**²⁸ (Fig. 3e). So "supply < demand" appears only if development is **frozen at today's projects** (~**2031**); with continued development—on a scale well within potential and supported by learning-curve cost declines and rapid deployment²⁹,³⁰—stranded supply keeps pace with demand. In other words, across the entire visible window that sets the build-out pace, watts are never the short side; the real speed limit is chips and capital.

### Why the token is the value-maximising export

Long-distance transmission is an engineering marvel; for connecting two strong grids it is often the right tool. But as a means of **monetising a resource with no local market**, it carries three structural disadvantages: physical loss (UHV export over 2,000–3,000 km typically loses 6–10%⁸,⁹); fixed capital for a commodity product (a multi-tens-of-GW corridor is a tens-of-billions-of-dollars dedicated asset whose unit cost roughly doubles if run at half its design utilisation, while delivered electricity is worth only ~$0.05–0.08 per source kWh¹⁰); and time (large lines often take nearly a decade from approval to completion, with over 2 TW of generation and storage already waiting in interconnection queues¹¹). Compute inverts this timeline—a data hall can be energised in months.

The older answer to monetising stranded power is to export an energy-intensive **product**. Primary aluminium is the archetype: Hall–Héroult electrolysis uses ~13–16 kWh of electricity per kg of metal, electricity is ~40% of the metal's cost, and aluminium is essentially "canned electricity"¹². This is exactly why smelters were deliberately sited next to otherwise-unsaleable hydropower¹²,¹³. We propose that the data centre is the modern smelter and the token the modern aluminium ingot—far denser, and with its vulnerability tellingly **inverted**: electricity is ~40% of aluminium's cost but only ~4–7% of compute's⁵. This cuts both ways: cheap power is a smaller share of the token business's advantage, but for that very reason the business is **robust** to energy prices rather than fragile.

The decisive quantity is the saleable value each export pathway packages into one source kWh (Fig. 4a). Delivered as an electron it is worth ~$0.05–0.08⁸,¹⁰; packaged into aluminium, ~$0.14–0.20¹²; converted to tokens, one kWh yields ~one million to ten million tokens—even sold at 2026's cheapest ~$0.4 per million tokens, the gross value is ~$0.7 per source kWh, and one-to-two orders of magnitude higher at frontier-model prices⁵,⁶. The token's gross value density is thus ~**one-to-three orders of magnitude** higher than the electron's.

But the cost side gives a finer—and more honest—conclusion. We accounted bottom-up for the life-cycle cost of a single accelerator's compute (the life-cycle cost of tokens, LCOT): at ~2 kW facility-level power and ~$50k fully-amortised cost per card, the mainstream tier (ε ≈ 2 J/token) yields a token cost of ~**$0.73 per million tokens**, of which chips and servers are ~58%, facility ~28%, and **electricity only ~6%**. A counter-intuitive corollary follows: cutting the electricity price from a grid-connected ~$60–90/MWh to a stranded ~$20–46/MWh lowers the cost per million tokens by only ~**3.1%**. In other words, the economic value of stranded resources lies **not in making tokens cheaper** but in two things unavailable elsewhere—monetising a large body of clean power that would otherwise be curtailed, and avoiding the large-scale transmission build that exporting electrons requires. Even after subtracting the 93–95% of revenue that pays for chips, facilities and labour, the residual (net) value per source kWh easily exceeds the entire landed value of an exported electron—on a life-cycle net basis the token is ~170× the electron and ~90× aluminium (because the electron and aluminium net values are near zero, this multiple should be read as an order of magnitude, ~50–170×), and beats both pathways across nearly the entire realistic price range (Fig. 4c).

### Chips, not watts, are the true scarcity

The same arithmetic that demotes cheap power to a secondary cost advantage also resets where the constraint lies. A ~1.2–2.3 PWh/yr portfolio corresponds to ~**130–260 GW** of continuous compute—requiring ~65 million to 130 million high-end accelerators (at ~2 kW facility-level per card¹⁵)—while global high-end accelerator output is only ~4–5 million/yr and is limited by advanced-packaging capacity¹⁶. Even allocating a fifth of world output to stranded sites deploys only ~2 GW of compute per year: a single 10-GW cluster takes ~5 years; filling 60 GW takes ~30 years, ~30 million accelerators and ~$1.5 trillion (~9× the dam); and filling the whole resource base is a multi-decade horizon.

A sensitivity analysis adjudicates where this constraint lies (Fig. 5). On the metric "years to fill 60 GW", perturbing each parameter in turn ranks them: **allocation share (swing ~4.8 yr) > packaging/CoWoS growth rate (~3.8 yr) > per-card power > accelerator output**; whereas sweeping **energy availability** from 1.16 to 2.25 PWh/yr swings it by exactly **zero**—because even its low end (~133 GW of potential) far exceeds the 60 GW being filled here. The shadow prices are equally lopsided: each +1% of global accelerator output adds ~0.02 GW/yr of deployable capacity, while each +1 PWh of energy adds ~0 GW—energy has zero marginal deployment value until it stops being abundant. Drawn as a phase diagram (Fig. 5b), the iso-GW contours are nearly **vertical** with respect to the energy axis: feeding even the most aggressive chip scenario needs only ~3.9 PWh/yr, far below the 1.2–2.3 PWh in hand. Extrapolating four named chip scenarios to 2050, the portfolio is **fully activated** in ~2034 (accelerated), 2037 (baseline), 2042 (export-restricted) and 2046 (constrained).

First movers must therefore be modest—a few GW co-located with one curtailed resource, not filling a whole dam at once. Stranded watts are abundant; the chips that consume them are the scarce input.

### What must hold, and what need not

The model fits a large and growing class of workloads, and its conditions are already met by several resources. **Batchable, delay-tolerant** work does not depend on proximity to users, and inference alone exceeded half of usage by end-2025⁵. The energy added by fibre is negligible and the latency added is only tens of milliseconds per thousand km—easily tolerated by such loads; cold, high-altitude sites are exceptionally favourable for cooling¹⁷; and Bitcoin mining has long shown that compute migrates as a "buyer of last resort" toward associated gas, curtailed hydro and surplus wind¹⁸. As for "why not just build at the demand side", the natural reply scopes the applicability: latency-sensitive and data-residency workloads should and will stay near users—our claim is only that the **batchable** portion has a strong, underrated reason to migrate to stranded energy, and that first-class resources should bring this option into planning view.

### Supply-side risks and limitations

The decisive risks are on the **supply** side. First and foremost is **temporal availability**: wind and solar are intermittent, and even a diversified portfolio has coincident troughs; our matching quantifies the residual firming need but does not eliminate it. Second is **seasonality and multi-day extremes**—dry seasons, winter solar lows, multi-day "wind-and-solar droughts", and climate-driven hydrological drought—which create sustained gaps that hourly storage cannot cover, falling instead on firm hydro and seasonal utilisation flex. Third is the **uncertainty of curtailment as a resource**: curtailment is by definition variable and shrinks as grids add storage and transmission, so compute sized to it must be interruptible. Fourth are **physical and logistical** limits—seismic hazard, cooling-water scarcity, high altitude, extreme remoteness, and the supply-chain problem of moving millions of accelerators into such places; the Yarlung Tsangpo site—designed for ~magnitude-8.5 earthquakes, nearly roadless, and on a disputed transboundary river—is an extreme instance of this class, not a template¹,². Fifth, **reliance on a grid or fossil backstop** would undercut both the "stranded" and "clean" premises. Two further points bound the climate claim: lowering token prices could, via a Jevons-style rebound, expand total energy and hardware use¹⁹; and accounting for "the clean energy embodied in exported compute" needs a carbon-accounting framework that does not yet exist²⁰.

### Outlook

The cleanest energy is increasingly stranded where no one can use it, while the fastest-growing demand for it can be consumed anywhere and shipped out as information. The two quantities match in scale: our tiered global stranded portfolio of ~**1.2–2.3 PWh/yr** is, in energy terms, about **1.2–2.3×** the IEA's 2030 AI-electricity projection (~1 PWh); at a mainstream energy-per-token (ε ≈ 2 J/token) it is enough to package ~**120–230×** the 2025 global token throughput (~1.75×10¹⁶/yr). Where these two facts meet, the optimal export path for a stranded watt is increasingly the token, and first-class stranded resources should be planned as compute terminals—as a complement to, even a substitute for, transmission. Four steps follow. Agencies should publish a "landed value per source kWh" metric, comparing electron, metal and token exports site by site, and extend it into a carbon-accounting framework for "the clean energy embodied in exported compute". Operators should pilot a gigawatt-scale, curtailment-financed inference cluster at an **existing** curtailed-renewable site—West Texas, northern Chile or Qinghai—as a near-term proof needing no Himalayan dam. Because the constraint is in silicon, chipmakers and policymakers should treat "build compute at the resource" as a chip-allocation and export-control question. And academia should standardise transparent disclosure of energy-per-token, because that parameter dominates every estimate here. Aluminium taught the twentieth century to can stranded power and ship it; in this decade, the densest can we have is the token.
