## Abstract

The world is building clean-power megaprojects in places with almost no one to use the electricity. China's Yarlung Tsangpo project, approved in 2024 and begun in 2025, will generate on the order of 300 terawatt-hours a year — about three times the Three Gorges Dam — in a remote Himalayan gorge more than 2,000 km from demand, where the local grid consumes only a few per cent of the output. The conventional answer is to move the electrons: thousands of kilometres of ultra-high-voltage line, with their losses, capital and dependence on demand at the far end. Here we argue that the highest-value way to export such stranded clean energy is to convert it on site into artificial-intelligence inference — tokens — and ship those over optical fibre. Electricity is only a few per cent of the cost of computation, yet a kilowatt-hour rendered as tokens can embed one to three orders of magnitude more exportable value than the same kilowatt-hour shipped as electrons, or than the aluminium that smelters have made from stranded hydropower for a century. At today's efficiencies a single such site could supply a large fraction to several multiples of the world's entire annual token demand, shifting the binding constraint from transmission to chips, capital and governance.

## Main

In July 2025, construction began on what will be the largest power station ever built. The Yarlung Tsangpo (Motuo/Medog) hydropower complex, on the great bend of the river that becomes the Brahmaputra, is designed around a roughly 2,000-metre natural drop and a cascade of underground stations with a planned capacity most commonly reported as about 60 gigawatts — credible analyses range higher, to 60–81 GW — and an expected output near 300 terawatt-hours per year, roughly triple the Three Gorges Dam¹,². It will cost on the order of 1.2 trillion yuan (~US$167 billion) and is not expected to generate before the early 2030s¹. Tibet, where it sits, consumes only about 12–13 TWh of electricity a year — a few per cent of the plant's output — and the nearest major load centres lie more than 2,000 km away across some of the most seismically active terrain on Earth².

The plant is therefore, from the moment it is switched on, a stranded-energy problem. The conventional framing treats this as a transmission challenge to be solved with more wire: under China's West-to-East Electricity Transmission programme, ultra-high-voltage (UHV) corridors would carry the power to the coast³. That framing is not wrong, but it is incomplete, and we believe it is increasingly the wrong default. The same decade that produced this dam has produced something else: an industrial demand for electricity that is unusually indifferent to *where* it is consumed, provided the result can be moved as information rather than as current. Global data-centre and artificial-intelligence (AI) electricity demand is rising steeply⁴, and the unit in which AI sells its output — the token, a fragment of text or other data produced by a large language model — has become both enormous in aggregate and, for the first time, credibly measurable in its energy intensity⁵,⁶. In 2025 the world's large-language-model providers processed on the order of 10¹⁶–10¹⁷ tokens, a figure that has grown several-fold per year⁷.

Here we argue that for premier stranded clean-energy resources the highest-value export is not the electron but the token: site AI inference at the resource and ship its output over optical fibre, rather than shipping electricity over lossy long-distance lines or wasting it as curtailment (Fig. 1). The case rests on three measured quantities that have only recently become large and well-characterised enough to compare directly — the levelized cost and stranding of remote clean power, the energy embedded per token of inference, and the global scale of token demand. We first show why moving electrons is a poor default for genuinely stranded resources; we then recover a century-old precedent — exporting stranded hydropower as energy-intensive products such as aluminium — and argue that the token is a far denser version of the same idea; we quantify the value-density gap and work through the Yarlung Tsangpo case; we map the global set of candidate sites and the conditions under which the model holds; and we confront its strongest objections, which are economic and geopolitical rather than physical.

### Moving electrons is the wrong default for stranded power

Long-distance transmission is a genuine engineering triumph, and for connecting a strong grid to another strong grid it is often the right tool. As a way of *monetising a resource that has no local market*, however, it carries three structural disadvantages. The first is physical loss. A modern ±800-kV UHV direct-current line loses on the order of 2.5–3.5% per 1,000 km along the conductor, to which must be added a distance-independent converter-station penalty of roughly 1.5% for the two terminals together; over the 2,000–3,000 km separating western generation from eastern demand, total losses of 6–10% are typical⁸,⁹. The headline figure of 1.5% per 1,000 km sometimes quoted for Chinese UHV refers to the world-leading ±1,100-kV class and is not representative of the fleet⁸.

The second disadvantage is that the capital is fixed and the cost per delivered kilowatt-hour therefore scales inversely with utilisation. A line sized for 60 GW of evacuation requires multiple ±800–1,100-kV corridors costing on the order of US$0.6 per watt, implying tens of billions of dollars of dedicated assets whose unit cost roughly doubles if they run at half their design utilisation — the historical norm for new western Chinese lines, which have also carried a majority of fossil rather than renewable power³,⁸. The third disadvantage is that the product delivered is merely a commodity kilowatt-hour: with a busbar cost near US$0.04/kWh for low-cost Chinese hydro¹⁰ and a levelized transmission adder of roughly US$0.01–0.03/kWh⁸,⁹, the energy arrives worth perhaps US$0.05–0.08 per source kilowatt-hour, and only if a buyer exists at the far end. For a resource whose local counterfactual is increasingly *curtailment* rather than sale — western Chinese provinces spilled double-digit percentages of their solar output in 2025, and reported Tibetan wind and solar curtailment exceeded 30%¹¹ — the transmission line is being asked both to be built ahead of the load and to find that load.

A fourth and deepest disadvantage is temporal. Transmission is slow: major lines take the better part of a decade to permit and build, and across the world the wires, not the turbines, have become the rate-limiting step of the energy transition — interconnection queues in the United States alone hold more than two terawatts of generation and storage waiting for grid access²⁴. Generation can be built far faster than the lines to evacuate it, so a stranded resource can spend years curtailed while its transmission catches up. Computation inverts this timeline: a data hall can be energised in months against a corridor that takes years, and it can grow incrementally with the resource rather than waiting for a single large line to be completed. There is an older answer to the monetisation problem, and a newer one; we take them in turn.

### Tokens as twenty-first-century canned electricity

For more than a century, regions with stranded hydropower have monetised it not by exporting electrons but by exporting an energy-intensive *product*. Primary aluminium is the canonical case: the Hall–Héroult process consumes roughly 13–16 kWh of electricity per kilogram of metal (global average ~14 kWh kg⁻¹), so that electricity is around 40% of the cost of the metal and the aluminium is, in effect, "canned electricity"¹²,¹³. This is why smelters were deliberately sited at otherwise unmarketable hydropower: Norway's electrometallurgical industry grew around remote falls from about 1905, and Iceland — an island with no grid connection to Europe and a tiny population — built dams such as Kárahnjúkar specifically to power smelters, so that today power-intensive industry, dominated by aluminium, consumes the majority of Icelandic electricity¹³,¹⁴.

The precedent carries a warning as well as a promise. Co-located energy-export industries live and die by cheap power: when electricity prices in the United States Pacific Northwest rose sharply at the turn of the millennium, the region's aluminium smelters — built on Bonneville hydropower — were idled within a few years¹⁴. An energy-export terminal is only as durable as its comparative advantage in cheap energy, which is precisely what a stranded mega-resource confers. We propose that the data centre is the modern version of the smelter, and the token the modern version of the ingot — but a far denser one. Where aluminium embeds the value of about 14 kWh into a kilogram of metal that must still be shipped physically, inference embeds the value of a kilowatt-hour into of order a million tokens that move at the speed of light over fibre, with negligible transport energy and no commodity-shipping logistics. The question is how much more value the token carries, and whether the resource can produce it at a scale that matters.

There is an instructive inversion in the analogy. Aluminium's fragility came precisely from electricity being so large a share of its cost: when power ceased to be cheap, the smelter died. Computation's electricity share is an order of magnitude smaller, which cuts both ways. It means cheap stranded power is a smaller part of a token operation's total advantage than it was for a smelter — but it also means such an operation is robust rather than fragile to energy prices, and that its decisive advantages are the two things a stranded resource uniquely supplies: a vast quantity of clean energy that would otherwise be curtailed, and freedom from the transmission build-out that monetising it as electrons would demand.

### Why the token wins: the quantitative core

**Sub-claim 1 · Value density — per source kilowatt-hour, a token is worth roughly 10–1,000× an exported electron and ~4–900× exported aluminium (Fig. 2).** The decisive quantity is the sellable value embedded in one source kilowatt-hour by each export pathway. Delivered as electrons, a kilowatt-hour of stranded hydropower is worth roughly US$0.05–0.08 at the far end of a UHV line⁸,¹⁰. Embedded in aluminium it is worth roughly US$0.14–0.20, given metal prices near US$2,600 per tonne¹²,¹³. Converted to tokens it is worth far more, for a reason that is initially counter-intuitive: electricity is only a small share of the cost of computation. Bottom-up accounting of AI inference puts hardware (servers and accelerators) at around 60% of total cost of ownership and electricity at only about 4–7%⁵. A kilowatt-hour therefore contributes only of order US$0.02–0.03 to the cost of the roughly one to ten million tokens it can produce, yet those tokens sell, even at 2026's cheapest budget rates of about US$0.40 per million, for of order US$0.7 per source kilowatt-hour — and for one to two orders of magnitude more at frontier-model prices. The gross value density of the token thus exceeds that of the electron by roughly one to three orders of magnitude (Fig. 2); even after the ~93–95% of revenue that pays for chips, facilities and labour, the residual value per source kilowatt-hour comfortably exceeds the entire delivered value of an exported electron.

![Figure 2 | Value-density ladder](figures/Fig2_value_density_ladder.png)

**Figure 2 | A token embeds energy at far higher value density than an electron or a metal.** Gross export value per source kilowatt-hour (log scale) for electrons via UHVDC (~US$0.05–0.08), aluminium (~US$0.14–0.20) and AI tokens (~US$0.7 at budget pricing to ~US$123 at frontier pricing), a spread of roughly one to three orders of magnitude.

**Sub-claim 2 · Output and global share — a single Yarlung-scale site could produce ~0.5× to ~30× the world's entire current annual token demand, and ~25–30× at central efficiency.** How many tokens could a flagship site produce, and how does that compare with global demand? The energy embedded per output token, ε, is the dominant uncertainty: datacenter-level measurements span from about 0.15–0.4 J per token for heavily batched inference on efficient hardware, through a central estimate near 2 J per token for a mainstream model, to 40–120 J per token for the largest frontier and reasoning-heavy workloads⁵,⁶. A 300 TWh-per-year site produces 3.6 × 10⁶ tokens per kilowatt-hour divided by ε; across the measured range this is 2.7 × 10¹⁶ to 1.5 × 10¹⁸ tokens per year (Fig. 3). Against a 2025 global throughput of order 1.5–2 × 10¹⁶ tokens per year⁷, a single site spans from roughly the entire world's current annual token output, in the pessimistic frontier-workload corner, to several tens of times it under efficient batched inference. We stress the honest reading: the *direction* is robust — one stranded mega-resource is comparable to or larger than today's whole token economy — while the *magnitude* is set by ε and should always be stated as a range. Demand is itself growing several-fold per year⁷, so this share will fall over time even as the absolute opportunity grows; the claim is about where value should be sited, not a static accounting.

**Sub-claim 3 · Cost structure — electricity is only ~4–7% of compute cost (~US$0.02–0.03 per million tokens), so the prize is avoided transmission, not cheaper tokens; the constraint becomes chips: filling 60 GW needs ~US$2.3 trillion, ~14× the dam (Fig. 4).** The same arithmetic relocates the binding constraint. Because electricity is a few per cent of compute cost while chips are most of it, cheap stranded power improves margins only modestly; what it removes is the *transmission* problem, not the *hardware* problem. Filling 60 GW with compute implies of order 40 million high-end accelerators and on the order of US$2 trillion of capital — roughly fourteen times the cost of the dam itself, and a large multiple of current annual global accelerator production. A flagship site is therefore not a switch to be thrown but a one-to-two-decade build-out, limited by chips, capital and logistics rather than by the physics of the watt. This is the sense in which our claim is marginal-economic and directional: at the margin, value should leave as tokens; at scale, how fast it can do so is a chip-supply question.

**Figure 4 | Token export shifts the binding constraint from transmission and energy to chips and capital.** (a) Bottom-up cost of ownership of AI compute — servers/chips ~60%, facility ~29%, electricity only ~4–7%. (b) Up-front capital to fill 60 GW with compute (~40 million accelerators, ~US$2.3 trillion) versus the dam (~US$167 billion), a ~14× ratio.

In practice the first movers will be modest. A few gigawatts of compute co-located with a curtailed-renewable resource — not a 60-GW dam filled at once — is the realistic near-term step, and it is already foreshadowed by hyperscale campuses now being announced at gigawatt scale and by the steady migration of energy-hungry computing toward the cheapest power. The Yarlung Tsangpo figure is therefore best read not as a forecast of one site's output but as a unit of measure: it shows that the stranded clean-energy resource base is, in token terms, comparable to the entire present-day AI economy, so that decisions about where the next gigawatts of compute are built are also, implicitly, decisions about which stranded watts get monetised and which are spilled.

### What would have to be true

The model is plausible for a large and growing class of workloads, under conditions that several flagship resources already meet. Inference and training that are *batchable and latency-tolerant* — large-scale pre-training, batch and reasoning inference, synthetic-data generation, and agentic background jobs — are precisely the workloads whose value does not depend on being physically near the user; reasoning-style workloads alone passed half of usage by late 2025⁵. Optical fibre adds negligible energy and only tens of milliseconds of latency per 1,000 km, which such workloads tolerate easily. Cold and high-altitude sites are unusually good for the dominant operational cost after hardware — cooling — with hyperscale operators already achieving power-usage effectiveness near 1.1 in cold climates¹⁵. And computing has already demonstrated its willingness to act as a flexible, location-agnostic, interruptible load wherever energy is stranded: bitcoin mining migrated to flared gas, curtailed hydro and oversupplied wind precisely as a buyer of last resort¹⁶, a proof of concept for siting compute at the resource even if its social value differs from that of AI.

Crucially, the case generalises well beyond one Chinese dam. Stranded and curtailed clean energy is a global phenomenon (Fig. 5): remote mega-hydropower from Grand Inga to Churchill Falls; structurally curtailed wind and solar in western China, west Texas, northern Chile, South Australia and Scotland; record-cheap desert solar in the Gulf, North Africa and Australia; and cold-climate hydro and geothermal in the Nordics, Canada, Iceland and the East African Rift, several of which already host data centres and energy-intensive industry on exactly the "canned-electricity" logic¹³,¹⁴. The distributed renewable sites in particular — where the local counterfactual is genuine curtailment and the jurisdictions are diverse — are the cleaner, more generalisable case for the argument than any single flagship.

The curtailed-renewable tier deserves particular emphasis, because there the argument needs no dedicated dam at all. Wind and solar that are routinely spilled — several terawatt-hours a year in west Texas, nearly a fifth of northern Chile's combined solar and wind output, and double-digit percentages across western China¹¹ — represent energy already built and already paid for, whose marginal value at the moment of curtailment is close to zero. Computation can act as an interruptible load that consumes this energy when it is abundant and yields it back to the grid when it is scarce, monetising the curtailed fraction without claiming the firm output that has genuine system value. This is the version of the idea that generalises most cleanly across diverse, democratic jurisdictions, and it is where a near-term pilot should begin. Box 1 details the Yarlung Tsangpo case as the extreme illustration: maximal scale and a cold, high-head resource, but also maximal physical and geopolitical difficulty.

The honest gaps are real. Cooling at altitude is harder as thinner air carries less heat; water for any wet cooling competes with other uses; the most spectacular resources are also the most seismically and logistically forbidding; and moving millions of accelerators into such places is a supply-chain problem in its own right. None of these is obviously disqualifying, but they bound where and how fast the model can be realised.

### Counterarguments, limits and risks

The strongest objection is that hardware, not energy, dominates the cost of computation, so cheap power at the source is a second-order advantage. We accept the premise and bound it: the value of stranded power is not mainly cheaper tokens but the conversion of an otherwise-curtailed or hard-to-evacuate resource into a globally tradable, high-value output without a transmission build-out. Because the capital and chips required dwarf the dam, the claim is explicitly about the *direction* of marginal value and a multi-decade trajectory, not about replacing a transmission line next year.

A second objection asks why not simply build the data centre near demand, where talent, supply chains and latency-sensitive customers are. Near-demand siting is indeed superior for those workloads and avoids the single-site geopolitical risks discussed below; our claim is not that all compute should move to the wilderness, but that the *batchable* fraction has a strong and underappreciated reason to migrate toward stranded energy, and that premier resources should be planned with that option in view. A related objection is that firm, dispatchable hydropower has high grid and system value that constant-baseload compute would waste. This opportunity cost is real wherever a grid can absorb the power; at a site whose local demand is a few per cent of output and whose 2025 curtailment exceeded 30%¹¹, that value is largely notional, but the argument would not hold at a well-connected resource, and the optimum is likely a hybrid of on-site compute, selective electron export and storage.

Further limits deserve explicit statement rather than rebuttal. Latency-sensitive and data-resident workloads cannot relocate at all. Water, altitude and extreme seismicity are first-order engineering risks at the most attractive sites. Making tokens cheap by tapping near-free energy may, by Jevons-style rebound, expand total AI demand and total energy and hardware use rather than reduce any footprint¹⁷; the framing monetises waste but does not by itself bound aggregate consumption. Most seriously, concentrating frontier compute at a single state-controlled site on a contested trans-boundary river compounds export-control, data-sovereignty, single-point-of-failure and downstream-water concerns for nations such as India and Bangladesh¹⁸. These are reasons to prefer the distributed, multi-jurisdiction version of the idea — curtailed renewables in Texas, Chile, Quebec and the Nordics — over the single most dramatic site, not reasons to dismiss the reframing.

A final objection concerns additionality and carbon accounting. If "stranded" power is in fact firm clean energy that could have displaced fossil generation elsewhere had a line been built, then converting it to tokens is not automatically climate-neutral; the environmental case is strongest where the energy is genuinely curtailed or where no evacuation route exists at all. Crediting a region for the energy embedded in exported computation — analogous to the embodied energy long recognised in traded aluminium — would require a carbon-accounting framework that does not yet exist. Building one is a prerequisite for treating token export as a clean-energy strategy rather than merely an economic one, and we return to it below.

### Outlook

The cheapest, cleanest energy is increasingly stranded where no one can use it, while the fastest-growing industrial demand for energy can consume it anywhere and ship the result as information. When those two facts meet, the best export route for a stranded watt is increasingly the token rather than the electron, and premier stranded-energy resources should be planned as compute terminals alongside, or instead of, transmission. We see four concrete next steps. Energy-system analysts and agencies should publish a delivered-value-per-source-kilowatt-hour metric that compares electron, metal and token export site by site, so that the choice is made on evidence rather than habit, and should extend it into a carbon-accounting framework that credits the clean energy embedded in exported computation — the embodied-energy gap flagged above. Operators should pilot a gigawatt-scale, curtailment-financed inference cluster at an existing curtailed-renewable site — west Texas, northern Chile or Qinghai — as a near-term proof that does not require a Himalayan dam. Chipmakers and policymakers should treat "compute at the resource" as a chip-allocation and export-control question, because that, not the watt, is the true constraint. And the field should standardise transparent disclosure of energy per token, since that single parameter, spanning two orders of magnitude, dominates every estimate in this Perspective. Aluminium taught the twentieth century to can its stranded electricity and ship it; within this decade, the densest can we have is a token.

## Box 1 | The Yarlung Tsangpo / Medog project

The Yarlung Tsangpo (Motuo/Medog) hydropower complex sits on the river's "Great Bend" in southeastern Tibet, where the Yarlung Tsangpo — which becomes the Brahmaputra downstream — drops roughly 2,000 m over a short distance before entering India. Approved in December 2024 and begun on 19 July 2025, it is built around diversion tunnels feeding a cascade of (reportedly five) underground stations. The most commonly cited planned capacity is about 60 GW, with credible estimates of 60–81 GW; expected annual generation is near 300 TWh, about three times the Three Gorges Dam, implying a capacity factor around 55%. Reported cost is ~1.2 trillion yuan (~US$167 billion), with first generation expected in the early 2030s. The engineering setting is extreme: the site lies near the Indus–Yarlung suture with design earthquakes around magnitude 8.5, in near-roadless terrain. The economic setting is equally striking: Tibet's local electricity demand is only ~12–13 TWh per year, a few per cent of the plant's output, the nearest large load centres are >2,000 km away, and no detailed power-evacuation plan had been published at groundbreaking — the conditions that make it the world's most dramatic stranded-energy resource and the natural, if most difficult, test of exporting energy as computation rather than as current.
